Background and Literature Review

Nurse Unionization, Staffing, and the Measurement Gap

Nurse Collective Bargaining in the United States

  • Nurse organizing accelerated after the 1974 NLRA amendments extended coverage to nonprofit hospitals (Becker, Sloan & Steinwald, 1982)
  • 27.8% of acute care hospitals have confirmed RN union representation (study panel: NLRB + FMCS + union lists); 13.2% of RNs report union membership (Bureau of Labor Statistics, CPS)
  • Major unions: NNU (175,000 RNs) (NNU, 2024), SEIU Healthcare, AFT Nurses & Health Professionals (120,000 RNs) (AFT, 2024)
  • CBAs are legally binding under the National Labor Relations Act, enforceable through grievance arbitration

Prior Research

  • Nurse staffing is associated with patient mortality and adverse events (Aiken et al., 2002; Needleman et al., 2002; Kane et al., 2007)
  • Unionized hospitals have higher RN-to-patient ratios (Ash & Seago, 2004; Dube, Kaplan & Thompson, 2016)
  • However, all studies treat unionization as a binary variable — union present or absent
  • Mechanisms through which unions affect outcomes remain insufficiently understood because contract content has never been measured (Dierkes et al., 2024)
  • Union membership alone is not associated with turnover, yet members report worse conditions (Soffen, 2026)
The relationship between unionization and outcomes is more complex than a binary variable can capture. No study has yet measured what nurse CBAs actually contain regarding staffing and patient safety provisions. This dissertation addresses that gap by developing the first provision-level measurement of CBA content and testing associations with hospital outcomes.

Methods — Paper 1

Theoretical and Conceptual Framework

Non-economic CBA provisions are conceptualized as negotiated governance structures — formalized arrangements that specify: (a) what standards or constraints apply, (b) who has authority to raise, review, or resolve operational issues, and (c) what documentation, reporting, or accountability procedures occur when standards are not met.

1. Institutional Theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983)

CBAs constitute one layer of the organizational governance environment. Provisions create formalized structures within hospitals.

2. Practice Environment Scale (PES-NWI; Lake, 2002)

Identifies nurse participation in hospital affairs, staffing adequacy, and nursing foundations for quality of care as key dimensions. CBA committees may function as formalized practice environment structures.

3. Professional Unionism (Freeman & Medoff, 1984; Clark et al., 2001)

Distinguishes the "monopoly face" of unions (wage effects) from the "voice face" (workplace governance). Non-economic CBA provisions represent the voice face.

Methods — Paper 1

Index Construction: Staffing Provisions Index (SPI) and Patient Safety Index (PSI)

Staffing Provisions Index (SPI): Ordinal 0–4

Based on the S1 × S3 interaction, reflecting the enforceability and specificity gradient:

ScoreConfigurationInterpretation
0No staffing provisionsNo contractual constraint
1Workload provisions only (S2)Indirect constraints
2Committee without binding standards (S3 only)"Process without power"
3Binding standards without committee (S1 only)Grievable but no forum
4Both standards and committee (S1 + S3)Grievable obligation + enforcement forum

Hospitals accumulate provisions in a Guttman-scale hierarchy (error rate 4.2%, Mokken H = 0.615). Where both S1 and S3 are present, they co-occur 93% of the time — supporting the complementarity hypothesis (cf. Ichniowski et al., 1997).

Patient Safety Index (PSI): Ordinal 0–2

Based on cumulative P1 + P2 specification:

ScoreConfigurationInterpretation
0No safety provisions (P1 or P2)No contractual safety governance
1P1 only (safety standards/ADO) or P2 only (safety forums)Partial: either protection or forum
2Both P1 and P2Standards + governance forum

P3 (reporting/accountability) and S2 (workload/assignment) enter outcome models as separate binary covariates, based on principal component analysis showing they capture independent dimensions.

Methods — Papers 2 & 3

Conceptual Framework

CBA provisions shape outcomes through structure → process → outcome (Donabedian, 2005). Each index operates through two mechanisms:

Staffing Provisions Index (SPI)
Direct: S1 standards specify ratios → binding obligation → workload constrained → nurses maintain vigilance
Voice: S3 staffing committees → nurses participate in staffing decisions → problems surfaced before they become crises
Patient Safety Index (PSI)
Direct: P1 ADO protections → nurses formally document unsafe assignments → accountability when standards not met
Voice: P2 safety committees → forum for safety governance → systemic risks surfaced before patient harm
↓                          ↓
Paper 2: Patient-level outcomes
Mortality, infections, patient experience
Paper 3: RN-level outcomes
Staffing intensity, vacancy, contract labor

SPI = S1 × S3 (standards × committees, ordinal 0–4). PSI = P1 + P2 (ordinal 0–2). S2 (workload procedures) and P3 (reporting) enter models as separate binary covariates — present in most contracts but low discriminant power.

Methods — Papers 2 & 3

Empirical Strategy

Primary: Correlated Random Effects (Mundlak, 1978)
  • Full sample: 197 hospitals, 1,152 hospital-years
  • Between + within-hospital variation
  • Hausman-type test for confounding
  • All estimates = associations
Feasibility: ICC = 0.61 (SPI), 0.68 (PSI); 34/96 hospitals changed index between contracts
Secondary: Staggered DiD (Callaway & Sant'Anna, 2021)
  • Subset: 96 hospitals with transitions
  • Treatment: CBA ratification timing (predetermined by expiration dates)
  • Cohort sizes: 4–5 hospitals
  • Results = suggestive corroboration

Sensitivity

Oster bounds (Oster, 2019) and E-values quantify unobserved confounding. Romano-Wolf stepdown correction (6 primary tests per paper).

How I built the measurement — and how I tried to stay honest about it

A walk-through of the coding process, the tools I chose, and the trade-offs

Everything so far was the committee version. For the next few slides I want to share the measurement engine — not as "the right way," but as the choices I made, why I made them, and what I'd flag for anyone coding text in their own work.
The starting dilemma

Why not all-by-hand, and why not all-by-machine

All by hand

  • 269,973 provisions across 607 contracts
  • At ~15 sec each → >1,100 hours
  • Not feasible for one person in a dissertation

All by machine, unchecked

  • Fast, but no human ground truth to check against
  • Hard to explain or audit a label later
  • Small errors would compound quietly across 270K rows
So I landed in the middle: hand-code enough to train and check, let the model draft the rest, and keep a human on every label. It's a practical compromise, with its own trade-offs.

Before any coding — getting the text out

Turning scanned PDFs into machine-readable text

Most of these contracts are scanned PDFs — essentially images. A computer can't read the words yet, so nothing downstream works until I fix that.

What I used: Docling

  • An open-source document parser
  • Runs OCR — turns the page image into real text
  • Keeps the structure: sections, paragraphs, and tables
  • Runs locally — contracts never leave the machine

Why it matters

  • Tables matter: nurse-patient ratios often live inside tables, not sentences
  • Outputs clean, structured text I can split into clauses
  • Quality gate: if a file yields zero clauses, it's flagged for manual review, not silently dropped
Pipeline: scanned PDF → Docling (OCR + structure) → structured text → split into clauses → classifier. This first step is easy to underestimate — but if it's wrong, everything after it is wrong.

What kind of "AI" this is

Not ChatGPT — a supervised classifier I trained myself

A point worth being precise about, because "AI" now usually means something else:

What I usedWhat people usually mean by "AI" today
ToolDistilBERT (a small, distilled BERT)ChatGPT / GPT-4 — large generative LLMs
What it doesReads a clause → labels it into my categoriesGeneral-purpose: generates text and reasons over it
How it learnsFine-tuned on my own hand-coded labelsGeneral pre-training I don't control
Where it runsLocally, deterministic, reproducibleCloud, general-purpose
Mine is a classifier: text in → a label from my codebook out. Not because it's better than an LLM — but because tying each label to categories I defined keeps the measure reproducible and auditable.
In methodological terms: this is a discriminative model — it learns P(label|text) from labelled examples. Generative LLMs learn P(text) and can reason broadly. The distinction matters for measurement: discriminative classifiers are tied to a defined construct and produce deterministic, auditable outputs. (Grimmer & Stewart, 2013 Political Analysis; Devlin et al. 2019 NAACL)
Training & evaluation

Turning hand-coded labels into a working classifier

What I trained on

  • Hand-coded corpus: 214 contracts · 85,629 provisions · 7 tags
  • Contract-level split: 182 contracts for training (85%) · 32 held out for evaluation (15%)
  • Model: DistilBERT (distilbert-base-uncased) fine-tuned on the training set
  • Runs fully locally — contract text never leaves the machine
  • Class-balanced: X0 = 86% of clauses → downsampled + per-class weighting to surface rare tags

How I tested it — deliberately strict

  • Contract-level split: 32 entire contracts held out (15%), coded from scratch
  • Not random sentences — whole contracts, never seen during training
  • Why stricter: a Kaiser clause reads differently from an ONA clause — held-out contracts test generalization across negotiating styles and union templates, not just unseen sentences from familiar contracts
Result: Micro F1 = 0.92 — the model agrees with my hand codes 92% of the time overall. But it is a draft tool: I confirm every label it produces. F1 measures draft quality and coding efficiency — not final label accuracy. (Final label accuracy = PI review, all 269,973 provisions.)

Devlin et al. 2019 NAACL (BERT) · contract-level split

Why I chose it — my decision, not a verdict on LLMs

An LLM would be a fine tool. For this project, this one was easier to defend.

Why a supervised classifier fit my case

  • Ground truth I own: trained on my own hand codes → I can trace & explain what it learned
  • Reproducible: same input → same output, so the audit trail stays clean
  • Runs locally: sensitive union contracts never leave the machine

To be clear

  • I'm not saying you can't use an LLM — you can, and many good studies do
  • It's a choice driven by my data (sensitive, needs auditability) and my need to defend every label
  • Different tools fit different questions
The principle underneath all of it

Reflexivity — staying aware that these are my judgments

Reflexivity is a core commitment in qualitative work: the researcher stays conscious of how their own assumptions and decisions shape the coding — and makes that process transparent. It isn't claiming "no bias"; it's building checks against my own judgment, as I go. (Lincoln & Guba 1985; Malterud 2001)

How I tried to practice it during the work, not after:

  • Member checking (2 rounds): I brought the taxonomy back to union staff (AFT + SEIU) to challenge my categories — they even proposed categories that turned out to have zero provisions, which told me the scheme was grounded in the text, not my assumptions (conventional content analysis; Hsieh & Shannon 2005)
  • Planned — second coder / ICR: my chair will code a stratified sample for inter-coder reliability (part of the verification plan, not yet done)
  • Audit trail: every coding decision version-controlled — traceable and reversible
  • Reporting my weak spots (next slide) rather than only the flattering numbers
Checking myself — honestly

What worked, and where it's weak

Classifier performance

  • F1 micro = 0.92 (agrees with hand codes 92% of the time)
  • F1 macro = 0.67 — lower, because rare categories are hard
  • Per-class: X0 = 0.96, S1 = 0.82 … P2 = 0.45
  • Checked on 32 held-out contracts hand-coded from scratch (true out-of-sample)

A tag is not yet a measure

  • "1:4" might be a clock time, a pay rate, a section number — not a staffing ratio
  • So the index uses mask-then-match: hide non-staffing numbers, then require a real ratio + binding language ("shall," "at all times")
  • The tag count is only an upper bound; the gated index is lower and cleaner

Being explicit about P2 = 0.45, and correcting my own inflated "binding" count down to an honest ~11–14%, is part of the same reflexive discipline.

What this process taught me

A few reflections — take what's useful for your own work

  • The tool doesn't have to be the newest thing — I chose a supervised classifier because it fit my data and let me defend every label. An LLM could work too; it's about the fit.
  • Automation is worth it, but I kept a human on every label I'd have to stand behind.
  • I stayed reflexive on purpose — member checking, a second coder, an audit trail, and reporting my weak numbers — because the categories are, in the end, my judgments.
Happy to talk more — about the classifier, the verification, or how any of this might apply to coding text in your own dissertations.
